# Fair sims warn twice before they punish

The design literature, the shipped games and the one controlled study all agree on a single test for whether a hard moment is fair: the player must have been able to see the cause before it hit, know which lever counters it, and have had time for that lever to work; fail any of the three and the loss is blamed on the game, which is what players mean by "unfair". Juul's 85-player study found that players who blamed their own mistakes rated the game more highly than those who called it too hard (p<0.016), and that players who never failed at all rated it lower, so "challenging but fair, not easier" is not a compromise but the position the evidence supports ([Juul](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)). Two Point Hospital earns that reputation not through gentle numbers alone but through a consistent grammar: every failure path has a graded prior signal (Close to Death before Dying, a warning at −$150,000 before bankruptcy at −$300,000), arrivals grow with the size of what the player built, per-event reputation changes are small (+1 cure, −2 death), and one star is enough to advance with the harder stars left as return-later goals ([TPH wiki: Death](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Death); [Money](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Money); [Hospitals](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Hospitals)). Across the wider genre the same patterns recur: bankruptcy is a two-condition trigger with a short, bounded grace rather than a number; the goals players like are plain counts with one visible quality floor, while derived metrics (hospital value, morale percentages, average grades) are the walls they complain about; demand follows reputation and built capacity under a soft cap; late-game money is kept meaningful by upkeep that scales with what was built; and studios find brittleness by comparing a thoughtless policy with a competent one across seeds and reading where losses cluster, not by reading a mean. Measured against this, every headline problem in the Pressure Point audit, the Orchard's guaranteed day-93 bankruptcy, the Boardwalk's annual summer collapse, the knife-edge reputation, the patience goals and the flat demand, is a legibility or feedback-loop problem rather than a magnitude problem, and each has a sourced fix that keeps the pressure while making it attributable.

## Attribution, not magnitude, decides whether a spike feels fair

The dividing line between hard and unfair is where the player places the blame. Juul's 2009 study asked directly whether players prefer games where they do not feel responsible for failing, and found the opposite: **players who attributed failure to their own mistakes rated the game more positively than those who said the game was too hard (p<0.016)**, while players who completed the prototype without ever failing rated it lower (p=0.06) and described it as shallow because nothing forced them to rethink ([Juul, "Fear of Failing?"](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)). Juul also recorded an attribution asymmetry, players credit themselves for success more readily than they accept blame for failure, which means a numerically symmetric reward and penalty is already experienced as tilted against the player ([Juul](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)). A larger retention study of over three million online-game data points reached the same shape of conclusion: self-attributed failure increased the likelihood of staying in the game while team-attributed failure decreased it ([ICIS / University of Miami](https://scholarship.miami.edu/esploro/outputs/conferenceProceeding/ICIS---The-Bright-Side-of/991032069202502976)).

The designer writing fills in what makes attribution possible. Soren Johnson's rule is that **"transparency is the antidote to suspicion and distrust"** and that "when the question is one of fairness, the player is always right"; explicit self-balancing rules such as Mario Kart's items or Catan's robber "don't feel like cheating because the game is so explicit about how the system works", whereas early Civilization AIs building under fog of war read as the computer "playing by different rules" ([Designer Notes, GD Column 7](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=132)). He adds two further conditions. Players "need to perceive that they are improving against a fixed measuring stick", so a world that silently scales with the player breaks the contract, and "if the player can see the invisible hand controlling the challenge, the spell is broken" ([Designer Notes, GD Column 10](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=179)). And timing matters as much as visibility: a hidden late "nasty surprise" leaves the player feeling "unprepared and cheated", unlucky outcomes "feel the fairest" when they arrive early enough to react to, and Civ4's decision to show the exact probability of combat success "drastically increased player satisfaction with the underlying mechanics" ([Designer Notes, GD Column 9](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=171)). Sid Meier's GDC 2010 formulation is the same idea from the player's side: "players want to be in control", "when random things happen, paranoia strikes the heart of the gamer", and after a setback "it's important to explain why these things happen, and how to avoid these things in the future" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/quotes-from-sid-meiers-keynote-gdc-speech)).

Keith Burgun names the structural failure. Output randomness is "noise injected between the player's decision and the outcome", severing the chain that lets a player learn from a result; input randomness is acceptable only when it lands far enough ahead that the player can respond, because an input "up so close in your face that you don't have time to respond to it" is as bad as a hidden roll ([Burgun, "Randomness and Game Design"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/randomness-and-game-design)). Daniel Cook generalises this to whole systems: players learn by forming and testing hypotheses, a system is "tight" when cause and effect are clearly linked, and the things that loosen it are long delays between cause and effect, indirection through secondary systems, hidden state and feedback that is "pretty but not functional"; he names feedback problems the most common design error and insists the lowest-skill loops should be the tightest "since they serve as the gateway" ([Lost Garden](https://lostgarden.com/2012/07/01/building-tight-game-systems-of-cause-and-effect/)). The practical test for a management-sim spike therefore has three parts: could an attentive player have seen the cause in the interface before it hit, did they know which lever counters it, and did they have enough days for the lever to bite.

Two Point Studios states its own position in exactly these terms and arrived at it by reacting to its earlier game. Gary Carr: **"Our games don't really punish you"**; he dislikes win criteria that block progression, and the studio's games "let you tinker with these little people and let you advance at whatever speed you wish" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-two-point-studios-carved-its-simulation-game-niche)). Ben Hymers framed the three stars as a progression system rather than a score: one star lets you advance but "more remains to do", and later unlocks can be carried back to make an earlier hospital's harder stars reachable ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2018/04/24/interview-two-point-hospital-devs-on-their-favourite-diseases-and-slapstick-vomit/)). Theme Hospital, by the same people in 1997, was the opposite model: each of twelve levels gated behind simultaneous targets that rise from **reputation 300, $1,000, 40% cured, 10 cures and $55,000 value at Toxicity to 800, $750,000, 70%, 750 and $250,000 at Battenberg**, with explicit loss conditions for negative funds and too many deaths, and a difficulty setting added only in a later patch ([StrategyWiki: Toxicity](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Theme_Hospital/Toxicity); [Battenberg](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Theme_Hospital/Battenberg); [Wikipedia: Theme Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theme_Hospital)). Battenberg also contains the genre's canonical unfair spike: two years of too few patients to fund growth, then "a sudden increase" producing long queues, deaths in the queue and epidemics at once ([StrategyWiki: Battenberg](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Theme_Hospital/Battenberg)). Both founders describe balancing the interlocking systems as the hardest and most fragile part of Two Point Hospital and say the studio is "not finished" at ship, which is the stance of a team that expects to tune in patches rather than lock numbers at release ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-two-point-studios-carved-its-simulation-game-niche); [TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2018/04/24/interview-two-point-hospital-devs-on-their-favourite-diseases-and-slapstick-vomit/)). One honest caveat: no controlled study of perceived fairness in management sims specifically was found, and Juul's data come from an arcade-style prototype; the transfer to tycoon games rests on the designer accounts and the shipped examples below.

## Shipped sims signal twice before any failure lands

Two Point Hospital's fairness is built from layered, graded warnings more than from soft numbers. A patient whose health nears zero shows a "Close to Death!" icon, and only "when it is too late" a "Dying!" icon "accompanied by the sound of a tolling bell"; a thirsty patient costs −1% happiness before "Very Thirsty" costs −12% per month; "Unhappy" precedes "About to rage quit"; and the money fail state is announced at −$150,000 before bankruptcy at −$300,000, at which point the player may reload or restart ([TPH wiki: Death](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Death); [Feelings](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Feelings); [Money](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Money)). Around those per-person signals sit queue warning icons over rooms with hover-to-highlight of the people waiting, heat-map overlays for attractiveness, temperature and hygiene in which failing machines "change from a dull Purple to bright Red", an advisor (Albert Crank) plus an inbox of letters, an always-visible objectives panel, and a money readout whose hover shows income, expenses and profit ([TPH wiki: Hospitals](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Hospitals); [Interface](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Interface); [MagicGameWorld UI guide](https://www.magicgameworld.com/two-point-hospital-ui-and-reading-your-menus/)). Debt itself is a graded consequence rather than a cliff: "while in debt, you cannot hire new staff" and "debt harms reputation" ([Gamepressure: finances](https://www.gamepressure.com/two-point-hospital/finances/z3b494)). The two-stage pattern is the game's main fairness device, and it is why forum veterans, not critics, were the ones calling the fail state unreachable.

Other sims encode "enough warning" in forms worth copying. Prison Architect's Alpha 10 replaced a hidden anger system with a "Thermometer" that shows **"the current temperature, rate of change and factors affecting the danger level"**, exactly the three things a slow decline needs to be attributable ([Prison Architect Wiki, Alpha 10](https://prison-architect.fandom.com/wiki/Alpha_10)). Its failure conditions pair a countable number with a stated consequence and a clock: twenty deaths in a day triggers a CEO phone call at midnight warning that five more the next day means game over; escapes work identically; bankruptcy gives 24 in-game hours to arrange a rescue package; counters are visible in the interface and "a game over countdown starts when you approach failure" ([Prison Architect Paradox wiki, Failure Conditions](https://prisonarchitect.paradoxwikis.com/Failure_Conditions)). Frostpunk uses a sustained threshold instead of an instant trip: the game ends only if Hope is too low or Discontent too high "for an extended period of time", citizens who intend to leave "first spend days trying to convince others", and the player is "notified you only have a few days to reverse the trend by accomplishing a specific goal" ([Wikipedia: Frostpunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/frostpunk-review); [RPG Site](https://rpgsite.net/review/7104-frostpunk-review)). Lead designer Jakub Stokalski describes the intended slide as "the analogy of the boiled frog: enough small, tenuous steps take us, as a society, to a very bad place", with the post-scenario summary asking "Was it worth it?" so the ending reads as consequence rather than punishment ([Gamepressure interview](https://www.gamepressure.com/editorials/fifth-dont-kill-on-moral-dilemmas-in-video-games/a-shard-of-ice-instead-of-a-heart/zf4b9)). The player-reported length of that ultimatum (two days) is not confirmed by an official source.

The SimCity lineage shows what fails. SimCity 2000's advisors "were poorly received by players as they only pop-up to give brief advice and complain"; SimCity 4's instead "notify through a pop-up dialog box, which will provide a link to direct to the scene of emergency" ([SimCity wiki](https://simcity.fandom.com/wiki/File:Ad.jpg)). A warning that names a symptom without pointing at a lever is noise. Cook ranks channels too: visual and tactile feedback is read more clearly than audio, tight designs stack aligned channels, and the designer should "make the most important information the center of attention, and remove distracting elements" ([Lost Garden](https://lostgarden.com/2012/07/01/building-tight-game-systems-of-cause-and-effect/)). Factorio's tutorial team wrote the most concrete channel discipline found: three channels with one purpose each, pausing speech bubbles "reserved for cases where the player must be told something", and an objective panel written so that "a player who skips the bubbles and story text should still be able to finish the level", after diagnosing that "too many messages add noise" and that information had been "scattered across the objective window, character chat messages, and yellow TAB bubbles" ([Factorio FFF-342](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-342); [FFF-241](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-241)). Synthesising these, the minimum legible package for any declining stat is its current value, its direction and rate, the top contributing causes, the threshold that matters, and a link to where the counter lives, surfaced while the player still has slack. Loss aversion is measurable even for virtual currency, players showed "a strong bias towards avoiding losses" of in-game gold, so framing a warning as "you are losing X per week" lands harder than "you earn Y less", and should be reserved for warnings the designer wants acted on ([University of Saskatchewan thesis](https://harvest.usask.ca/handle/10388/13478)).

## Bankruptcy is a two-condition trigger with a bounded grace, not a number

The genre almost never ends a game at zero cash. Prison Architect's sandbox bankruptcy requires **both a negative bank balance and negative cash-flow**, then grants 24 in-game hours to arrange a rescue, with a Government Bailout available once per game and a researchable "Legal Defense" that averts game over a second time; campaign mode cannot be failed at all ([Prison Architect Paradox wiki, Failure Conditions](https://prisonarchitect.paradoxwikis.com/Failure_Conditions)). The order in which Introversion did this matters: the Alpha 20 fail states were added only after the previous alpha reworked finances "so funds flow better", and they were switched off for every pre-existing prison ([GameWatcher](https://www.gamewatcher.com/2014-29-04-prison-architect-alpha-20-introduces-failure-grading-and-player-jail-time)). Cities: Skylines has no bankruptcy; when money falls below −₡10,000 a one-time ₡50,000 bailout is offered, interest-free and unrepayable, at the cost of disabling achievements for that save, and three loans (₡20,000 at 5%, ₡60,000 at 10%, ₡200,000 at 15%) unlock with city milestones ([Cities: Skylines Paradox wiki, Economy](https://skylines.paradoxwikis.com/Economy)). RollerCoaster Tycoon fails only by missing the objective at a deadline; company value "can even be a negative value and you will still win as long as the objective is met" ([RCT Fandom, General Scenario Guide](https://rct.fandom.com/wiki/General_Scenario_Guide)). Two Point Hospital's loans are likewise tiered to size: $25,000 to $50,000 at 5%, $75,000 to $100,000 at 10%, and $150,000 to $250,000 at 15% gated behind a $500,000 hospital value, 25 reputation and hospital level 2 ([TPH wiki: Loans](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Loans)). The cushion in every case is a fixed sum plus a fixed window plus a one-shot, which keeps it from becoming a strategy; where a sim has no money game-over, it substitutes softer costs such as blocked building while in the red, compounding interest or a closed park. The one counter-example is instructive: Game Dev Tycoon's bankruptcies, described as "surprisingly easy", come from fixed costs committed before revenue (moving office and hiring before building capital), and the developer's response was a tips post rather than a rule change ([Pure Nintendo](https://purenintendo.com/review-game-dev-tycoon-nintendo-switch/); [Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/239820/discussions/0/864977026045070690)).

Failure that teaches, rather than ends, is the evidence-backed target. Juul's data show failure "adds content" by forcing players to reconsider strategy, while the energy-punishment variant of his prototype did not make players like it more, and he attributes casual games' appeal to sparing "setback punishment" such as forced replays ([Juul](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)). Johnson says the same of repetition: forcing a player back through mastered sections means "the game is punishing the player instead of challenging him" ([Designer Notes, GD Column 10](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=179)). A hard game-over is defended where the theme is the consequence itself, as in Frostpunk's banishment, and where the game has shown the cause and given time to act ([Slant](https://www.slantmagazine.com/games/review/frostpunk)). Schell's Lens of Punishment asks the questions a designer should put to every penalty: "Do my punishments seem fair to the players? Why or why not? Is there a way to turn these punishments into rewards and get the same or a better effect? Are my strong punishments balanced against commensurately strong rewards?" ([Schell Games](https://schellgames.com/blog/the-lens-of-punishment)).

On asymmetric penalties the literature gives no ratio but a clear set of conditions. Johnson observes that Civilization's small incremental AI bonuses drew little anger because "their effect is too small to notice on a turn-by-turn basis", while granting a Wonder instantly "felt entirely different", and that the human mind "is notoriously bad at grasping probability", so a penalty is tolerated when it is announced in advance, small per tick and attributable to a decision, and resented when it lands as one unforeseen lump ([Designer Notes, GD Column 7](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=132)). Positive feedback loops are the engine of death spirals and negative loops the stabiliser, but hidden stabilisers read as cheating; the fix is to make the loop explicit, as Civ4 did with "a per-city maintenance cost that scaled with the total number of cities" to kill infinite city sprawl ([Designer Notes, GD Column 17](https://designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-17-water-finds-a-crack); [Chemeketa reader](https://computerscience.chemeketa.edu/cis125greader/MechanicsDynamics/FeedbackLoops.html)). Two shipped systems show explicit anti-spiral rules in numbers. RimWorld's adaptation factor starts at 0.8, cannot rise for the first 30 days, loses 30 "AdaptDays" per colonist death and 3.5 to 8 per downing, and is clamped to 0.4 to 1.47, so a colony that has just taken losses faces smaller raids by rule ([RimWorld Wiki, Raid points](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Raid_points)). Valve's Left 4 Dead mob size "grows from minimum just after spawn to maximum after a duration to balance difficulty of successive, frequent Mobs", so back-to-back events cannot compound ([Valve L4D slides](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf)).

Reputation deserves its own numbers because it is where Pressure Point's spiral lives. Two Point Hospital's wiki-measured deltas are **+1 for a cure, −1 for a failed treatment, −2 for a death, −0.25 for sending a patient home, −0.5 for a rage quit and −0.25 for waiting too long**, which is why a hospital curing half a large stream still gains reputation on net and why veterans complained it had "no real effect" ([TPH wiki: Hospitals](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Hospitals); [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/535930/discussions/0/2590022385666162871)). CorsixTH, the open-source Theme Hospital, models reputation on a 0 to 1000 scale with +1 cure, −4 death, −3 sent home, +15/−20 for emergencies and −50 for a room crash, but the important feature is the dampening: "under 500, a gain is always effective; over 500, a loss is always effective", with other changes applied at a probability that falls to 40% near 100 or 900 and zero at the ends ([CorsixTH hospital.lua](https://github.com/CorsixTH/CorsixTH/blob/master/CorsixTH/Lua/hospital.lua)). That rule makes the bottom of the scale a floor the player can climb out of and the top a ceiling that resists complacency. These constants are the clone's own approximations, flagged in-source as needing difficulty dependence, not Bullfrog's verified numbers, but the shape is the point: both hospital games price a death at roughly two to four cures and dampen toward the extremes, so death is a nudge rather than a cliff.

## Plain counts with one visible floor outlast derived-metric goals

The objective shapes that have survived 25 years of tycoon campaigns are plain counts the player can watch tick up, bundled three or four at a time with one soft quality gate. Two Point Hospital's first star is "cure 3 Lightheadedness patients"; its final base-game level asks for 1,000 cures, a 90% cure rate, 90% reputation and $10M value, and is described as having "the maximum number of patients eventually arriving" plus every disaster and VIP ([TPH wiki: Hogsport](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Hogsport); [Croquembouche](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Croquembouche)). Theme Hospital's first level is "an easy one - you've simply got to cure ten patients" ([StrategyWiki: Toxicity](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Theme_Hospital/Toxicity)). RollerCoaster Tycoon pairs a guest count with a park rating floor by a date (Forest Frontiers: 250 guests and 600 rating by the end of Year 1; Thunder Rock: 900 and 600 by Year 4), stretching deadlines along with targets ([Giant Bomb: RCT](https://www.giantbomb.com/games/3030-2236/)). Satisfactory forces the order of its six Tier 0 upgrades and then frees it ("within an open tier, you can complete milestones in any order"), locking sequence only while the player cannot yet judge priorities ([Satisfactory Wiki: Tiers](https://satisfactory.wiki.gg/wiki/Tiers)). Factorio's tutorial redesign set the rule that each level should "start where the previous one ended" so a build is never thrown away, and that objectives should "have clear purpose, tied to progression rather than handed out as chores" ([Factorio FFF-241](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-241)).

The goals players name as walls are, with remarkable consistency, derived metrics whose formula they cannot see. Two Point Hospital's Flemington thread turns on a $5M hospital value and cure counts for rooms whose research may not be done, with a player whose value "peaked around $500k, then fell below $200k" ([Steam: Flemington](https://steamcommunity.com/app/535930/discussions/0/1737715419909396845)); Grockle Bay's hospital value "kept slipping" through repeated bankruptcies because value "depends on rooms, consecutive profitable months, and cash on hand, so staying in the red drags it down" ([Steam: Grockle Bay](https://steamcommunity.com/app/535930/discussions/0/3104564981114190944)); Duckworth's 90% staff morale target is "very hard" and was beaten by raising pay heavily right before sending staff on break to spike the number past the line ([Steam: 1* or 3*](https://steamcommunity.com/app/535930/discussions/0/2590022385665527144)). Two Point Campus repeats the pattern with its average-grade goals: a reviewer met a B+ target "due to attrition" after two students dropped out, could not see why students felt bored or unhygienic despite the facilities, and named "lack of information" as the source of frustration ([Jump Dash Roll](https://jumpdashroll.com/article/two-point-campus-review)). Each community workaround (pre-researching on earlier maps, hoarding cash before expanding, spiking morale, relying on dropouts) games the metric rather than improving the hospital, which is the signature of an opaque objective. **No example was found of a tycoon studio rewriting a specific star goal after launch**; the documented post-launch changes are to access and difficulty settings (Frostpunk unlocking all scenarios after 20 days and adding Extreme; Factorio replacing its tutorial twice) ([GOG DB: Frostpunk notes](https://www.gogdb.org/product/1648559910/releasenotes); [Factorio FFF-342](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-342)).

Progress communication has had the same floor since 1997: a per-criterion bar with a hover giving have and need. Theme Hospital's status screen tooltips tell you "how much you have and how much you need to get 100 percent in that criteria" ([Force For Good](https://forceforgood.co.uk/strategy/theme-hospital/)); Two Point Campus's grade objective hover "reveals at least seven factors that will affect your students' average grades" ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/two-point-campus/grades)). What separates a readable goal from an opaque one is whether the bar's input is itself legible: cures and guests are, value and averages are not unless the factor breakdown is shown. No source described a near-completion cue; nudges are stall-oriented (lost guests, blocked construction, dying patients), so a sampled or averaged goal that slips reads to the player as the goal moving.

Pacing has two documented escalation models. The hospital games add a system per level (epidemics, VIPs, inspectors, weather, a rival), which produces "I didn't know that mechanic existed" complaints; RollerCoaster Tycoon keeps the systems and changes the constraints (space, terrain, inherited problems, money limits that are "the one restriction usually not stated up front"), which produces hidden-limit complaints ([Shamus Young](https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=57879)). Two Point Campus's most consequential change was to the cadence itself: an academic year of roughly twenty minutes opens with a summer break in which the player adjusts the campus before students arrive, and intake is spent, not inflicted, through Course Points earned ten per campus level and used to upgrade courses that admit more students, with guides warning not to raise intake "until you're very comfortable with your campus" ([Wikipedia: Two Point Campus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Point_Campus); [GamesRadar: Course Points](https://gamesradar.com/two-point-campus-course-points)). Frostpunk shows the limit of acceptable stacking: The Fall of Winterhome "throws many at once" and is accepted because a doomed city is the premise, while the base game's scripted Hope drop drew the complaint that "it really takes away from the game to have an automatic defeat event that will hit no matter what", precisely because it arrived after a player had every visible stat under control ([COGconnected](https://cogconnected.com/2018/09/frostpunk-fall-winterhome-impressions/); [Steam: Winterhome](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1696045708646208587)).

## Demand follows capacity and reputation, and late money needs a sink

In Two Point Hospital arrivals are not a function of reputation alone. The maximum number of patients present "at any one time [is] determined by the hospital's level and reputation", and Hospital Level "is a measure of the overall size of your hospital based on the rooms and staff", capped at 30 and opening a new applicant slot every five levels ([TPH wiki: Hogsport](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Hogsport); [Hospitals](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Hospitals)). Building more rooms therefore brings more patients, and the loop is self-reinforcing once cash-positive. RollerCoaster Tycoon makes the same coupling explicit in code: spawn probability runs from 50 at park rating 200 up to 700 at 850, is divided by four once guests exceed a "suggested guest maximum" built from per-ride bonuses (a giga coaster adds 120, a corkscrew 100), divided by four again for an entrance fee out of line with ride value, and multiplied by 1.25 per positive award, while marketing campaigns add a separate spawn probability that bypasses the cap ([OpenRCT2 guest spawn guide](https://forums.openrct2.org/topic/2861-guide-when-and-how-do-guests-spawn/)). Planet Coaster 2 adds an anti-specialisation rule: "your Park Rating will be determined by your lowest performing area" ([Frontier, Deep Dive](https://www.planetcoaster.com/news/2024-09-25/deep-dive-mastering-management)). Prison Architect and Banished make intake a player decision: a per-category intake tab, and nomad groups of 10 to 30% of population that can be refused, with later groups smaller after a refusal ([Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/pc/prison-architect-how-to-build-a-good-prison-and-keep-prisoners-happy/); [Banished wiki: Nomad](https://banished-wiki.com/wiki/Nomad)). The recurring failure in demand design is an invisible cap: an OpenRCT2 player with rating 999 and a stagnating park read it as a broken algorithm until others traced it to the dominant guest thought "I'm running out of cash" with no cash machines ([OpenRCT2 forums](https://forums.openrct2.org/topic/6179-guest-generation-algorithm-changed-cant-finish-scenario-without-advertising-anymore/)); SimCity 4's per-tile demand caps have no in-game readout, and players report demolishing 50,000 population with "no change in RCI Demand" ([Steam SC4](https://steamcommunity.com/app/24780/discussions/0/496880503057331341)).

Two Point Hospital's "money too easy" complaint is a story about what money measures. A guide states that "the only stable source of income are the treatments the patients receive", that prices can be raised at the cost of reputation and happiness, and that "higher prices lower reputation" ([Gamepressure: finances](https://www.gamepressure.com/two-point-hospital/finances/z3b494)). Veterans nonetheless reported "patients dying left and right, yet you still make money and win the level", a pricing exploit of raising everything to bank cash and lowering it once built out, a leaderboard hospital valued at $125M earning about $22M a year on a low cure rate, and the observation that with a cure-percentage objective "you can send patients home and only cure the easy ones" ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/535930/discussions/0/2590022385666162871); [Steam thread 2](https://steamcommunity.com/app/535930/discussions/0/1743352529779588584)). Critics largely did not call it easy, and one player's counterpoint was that "there comes a point in the game where the number of patients you treat cannot compensate the expenses" ([Steam thread 2](https://steamcommunity.com/app/535930/discussions/0/1743352529779588584)). The studio's own view is that payment was there "to give players rewards", not to simulate a health system ([New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/?p=221062)). No developer statement acknowledging the complaint was found.

The levers other sims use to keep late money meaningful are upkeep that scales with the thing built, threat that scales with wealth, soft caps on demand, and debt with real interest. Cities: Skylines II's Economy 2.0 removed government subsidies "to make the economy more challenging and transparent" and added a Tile Upkeep cost for owned land that "hits hardest for cities that unlocked most of the map early" ([Paradox](https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/cities-skylines-ii/news/economy-patch-is-here)); the base game's service budgets follow a quadratic curve in which the last five points add only about 0.2% of effect ([Cities: Skylines wiki, Economy](https://skylines.paradoxwikis.com/Economy)). RimWorld is the only game found that makes money itself dangerous, with raid points equal to (wealth points + pawn points) × difficulty × starting factor × adaptation, and it hedged every edge: wealth points flatten at 4,200 above 1M wealth, raid points cap at 10,000, buildings count at half weight, and a "Wealth Independent" mode replaces wealth with a timer for players who found the coupling perverse; players still complained that scaling is "based essentially entirely on wealth multiplied by difficulty" ([RimWorld Wiki, Raid points](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Raid_points); [Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/294100/discussions/0/3160958175962150146)). The designer response that stuck, across RimWorld, CS2 and Prison Architect 2's sandbox toggles, was exposure and opt-outs rather than cushions.

Seasonal and surge demand is made fair by telegraphing it and handing the player a throttle. Frostpunk announces its final storm in advance and expects stockpiled coal and steam cores and automatons on the mines ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/tips/frostpunk-how-survive-storm)); Banished lets the player refuse a nomad wave outright; RollerCoaster Tycoon lets the player both pull guests in with campaigns and push them away with the entrance fee, which is why its crowds rarely become a spiral ([OpenRCT2 guest spawn guide](https://forums.openrct2.org/topic/2861-guide-when-and-how-do-guests-spawn/)). Where a surge is scripted and untelegraphed, as with Frostpunk's Winterhome Hope drop, players call it "an automatic defeat" ([Steam: Winterhome](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1696045708646208587)). Cities: Skylines II's post-patch "death wave" is the same lesson from the other side: a one-time surge in deaths over one to two months that Paradox fixed by spreading death ages so "you don't end up with a very large amount of seniors who all die around the same time" ([Paradox](https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/cities-skylines-ii/news/economy-patch-is-here)). The fair versions all share advance notice, a pre-commitment tool and a cost to the counter.

## Bots expose brittleness only when you compare policies and seeds

No studio or paper uses a single fairness metric; the working consensus combines an outcome rate, a comparison across skill tiers or agent types, and a distribution of where and why losses happen. Politowski et al. assess balance with reinforcement-learning agents on two axes, "Challenge vs. Success" (novice against professional agents across versions to find spikes) and "Skill vs. Chance": **if a random agent scores as well as trained agents and humans, the version is luck-dominated**, and in their Batkill test the random agent matched or beat trained agents on two versions, flagging them, while in Jungle Climb it scored far below on every version ([arXiv 2304.08699](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08699)). The same paper tunes by "Doubling and Halving", changing attributes by large amounts "so the difference is noticeable" before measuring, and recorded a designer expectation falsified by data when an added hazard failed to make the game easier as expected ([arXiv 2304.08699](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08699)). Google's Chimera study defined imbalance as deviation from an intended win rate (59.1% against 40.9% across a million self-play games where parity was intended) and retuned until the split was 49.8/50.2 while also raising the average number of evolves per game, itself a design goal, claiming the imbalances were found "in a span of days" ([Google Research](https://www.research.google/blog/leveraging-machine-learning-for-game-development/)). Riot's Legends of Runeterra agent reached 48% against top humans and its balance scores "consistently matched design intuition" and became designer KPIs, though the account is a vendor summary ([Anyscale](https://www.anyscale.com/blog/riot-games-and-deep-reinforcement-learning-in-gaming)). Mega Crit published Slay the Spire 2's open-beta rate (60.5M wins in 240M runs, 25%) sliced per character, and its GDC talk warns about "data deception" and "super playtester" bias, the distortion of balance readings by the strongest testers ([GameGeeker](https://gamegeeker.com/fr/news/slay-the-spire-2-may-neows-news-update?market=FR); [Podwise outline](https://podwise.ai/episodes/1459444)). The mean is a target; the slices are the diagnosis.

Where losses happen is the metric most tied to perceived fairness. Valve plots deaths over a level map to see clustering ([UBC lecture deck](https://cmps-people.ok.ubc.ca/bowenhui/game/lectures/6-metrics.pdf)); the Hochschule Trier "death-related problem level likelihood indicator" quantifies both the magnitude and the density of deaths to find chokepoints and "correlated to enjoyment ratings for the game levels" ([Informatik-Bericht 2015-01](https://www.hochschule-trier.de/fileadmin/Hauptcampus/Fachbereich_Informatik/Forschung/Informatik_Berichte/2015/Informatik-Bericht-2015-01.pdf)). Assembled from these building blocks, and not published as such by any studio, a seed-variance protocol is: fix a policy, run many seeds, and report the full distribution of time-to-goal and time-to-loss rather than the mean; a bimodal curve (many early losses and many wins) is a cliff, a hazard rate that climbs steadily after a first adverse event is a spiral, and a seed on which both a thoughtless and a competent policy die is unwinnable rather than hard.

Strong bots and human-like bots answer different questions. Self-play agents find exploits: Supercell's mo.co bots "gravitated toward particular items", flagging them as overpowered, with a new model trained every week or two ([PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/how-moco-used-ai-to-test-the-player-experience/)). Human-like bots predict experience: King's Candy Crush bot "opts for the move most likely chosen by a human, based on behavior data from millions of game states", plays a level over a thousand times and reports "Is this playable? How much of a challenge is it to the player?" ([Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/?p=3565450)). Without player data, Holmgård's procedural personas are the substitute: the same search algorithm with different utility weights (a Runner, a Monster Killer, a Treasure Collector, a Completionist) produced different win rates per map and metrics that correlated with level structure, with the authors' caveat that personas are only as honest as the designer's weights ([arXiv 1802.06881](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.06881)). Valve's SurvivorBots served as "Competent Human Player Proxies" enabling "automated stress testing with 4 SurvivorBots and accelerated game time" ([Valve L4D slides](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf)). The limits are real: optimal bots over-report exploitability and under-report confusion, and Johnson's experience is that "when the public gets the game, they find problems that you never did" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/design-success-means-knowing-what-to-do-with-feedback)).

Two further lessons transfer directly. Valve's Director "adjusts pacing, not difficulty. Amplitude (difficulty) is not changed, frequency (pacing) is", a crude intensity estimate "works", and scripted encounter placement failed because "players are good at memorizing all static locations" ([Valve L4D slides](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf)). An adaptive system that lowers event frequency after a loss avoids a spiral without touching the fairness contract; one that silently lowers amplitude makes outcomes unattributable. And difficulty knobs are exposed without fragmenting the design by one trick: a single table of per-system values, presets as rows, custom screen as the table. RimWorld's six presets are rows of about thirty values (threat scale 10 to 220%, adaptation impact 100 to 40%), its storytellers change variance not mean (Randy multiplies each event by 50 to 150%), and its Commitment toggle is kept orthogonal ([RimWorld wiki: Difficulty](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Difficulty); [Raid points](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Raid_points)). Frostpunk 2 exposes four per-system sliders (Economy, Weather, Frostland, Society) locked once the mission starts ([Escapist](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/all-frostpunk-2-difficulty-settings-explained/)). Oxygen Not Included's "No Sweat" preset explicitly leaves oxygen, temperature and building ratios untouched, a statement of which pressures define the game ([ONI wiki](https://oxygennotincluded.wiki.gg/wiki/Game_Settings)). Two Point Campus's sandbox has a Challenge mode with "a challenging balance sheet to manage" alongside Creative and Custom ([Gamer Guides](https://gamerguides.com/two-point-campus/guide/getting-started/basics/sandbox-mode)), and Celeste's Assist Mode acts on the player's capabilities rather than the world's pressures, with the in-game text recommending the tuned default first ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/celeste-difficulty-assist-mode/)).

## What this implies for Pressure Point Institute

The audit measures the campaign against "about as hard as a Two Point Hospital campaign: easy to get going, demand that outruns the player, money rarely the worry early on, a reputation that moves slowly, and pressures that arrive one at a time" ([deep-dive audit](deep-dive-audit.md)). Read against the findings above, each of its headline problems fails one of the three attribution conditions (seen, understood, time to counter) or is an explicit positive feedback loop, and each has a sourced pattern that fixes the legibility or the loop without lowering the pressure.

Three of these deserve more than a row. The Orchard is the clearest case of a loss that fails all three attribution conditions at once: the payday rule arrived in 0.30.14, the bot's reasonable opening dies on the same day in every seed, and nothing in the interface shows projected payroll against projected takings. Prison Architect's sequence is the model: Introversion reworked finances "so funds flow better" first, added failure afterwards, and defined it so that a one-off purchase that dips the balance while income is positive can never trigger it ([GameWatcher](https://www.gamewatcher.com/2014-29-04-prison-architect-alpha-20-introduces-failure-grading-and-player-jail-time); [Prison Architect wiki](https://prisonarchitect.paradoxwikis.com/Failure_Conditions)). The audit's own first item, telling the player as soon as projected payroll outruns projected takings and putting overdraft interest, morale and resignations on the way down, is this pattern; the research adds that the cushion should be a bounded, visible grace (a count of days, a one-shot bank intervention) rather than a larger number, that loans should scale with the center as Two Point's three lenders do, and that the loss condition should require negative cashflow as well as negative balance. The "with bankruptcy off, three stars by month 17 to 20" result is the mark to keep: Juul's and Johnson's evidence says a player should still be able to lose the Orchard, only to a mistake they can name.

The reputation rule is a positive feedback loop with a hidden shape, and the literature is unanimous that hidden loops are what players resent. The current taper applies to gains only, so the system has a ceiling but no floor; CorsixTH's clone of Theme Hospital shows the symmetric design, with gains always effective below the midpoint and losses always effective above it, and both dampened toward the ends ([CorsixTH hospital.lua](https://github.com/CorsixTH/CorsixTH/blob/master/CorsixTH/Lua/hospital.lua)). Two Point's magnitudes (+1, −2) are the other end of the range and drew the "no real effect" complaint, so the owner is right not to copy them wholesale; what should be copied is the two-stage signal before each loss. Today a guest who gets worse costs −6 and nothing warns that a line is about to produce one; Two Point shows "Close to Death" before "Dying" and a queue icon before either ([TPH wiki: Death](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Death); [Hospitals](https://two-point-hospital.fandom.com/wiki/Hospitals)). A reputation panel that shows value, rate of change, the top causes this month and the threshold that matters, the Prison Architect thermometer transposed, is the minimum; Frostpunk's sustained-threshold rule suggests that reputation effects on arrivals should lag a little so a bad week is a warning and a bad quarter is the consequence.

The patience goals and the send-home dominance are one problem seen twice, and Two Point's own forums document the same exploit under a cure-percentage objective ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/535930/discussions/0/2590022385666162871)). Johnson's "water finds a crack" is the general law: "given the opportunity, players will optimize the fun out of a game", and a dominant strategy removes choice because alternatives become "provably sub-optimal" ([Designer Notes, GD Column 17](https://designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-17-water-finds-a-crack)). Counting sent-home guests in the denominator and replacing the patience percentage with a count or a wait in days (RollerCoaster Tycoon's guests-plus-rating shape) restores the choice. The Exchange's star ordering is a smaller instance of the same legibility rule: Satisfactory's forced Tier 0 exists so that a player who cannot yet judge priorities is not asked to, and a second star whose inputs arrive after the third's asks exactly that.

The testing implication is that the repository already holds the right instrument and is missing two readings. The bot that plays every center on three seeds for four years is Valve's "competent human player proxy" with accelerated time ([Valve L4D slides](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf)). What Politowski's method adds is a second, thoughtless policy run over the same seeds: if it dies where the competent bot dies, the seed is unwinnable rather than hard, and "bankrupt on day 93 in every seed" with zero timing variance is the cliff signature the death-density literature describes ([arXiv 2304.08699](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08699); [Informatik-Bericht 2015-01](https://www.hochschule-trier.de/fileadmin/Hauptcampus/Fachbereich_Informatik/Forschung/Informatik_Berichte/2015/Informatik-Bericht-2015-01.pdf)). The second missing reading is the distribution: per-seed survival curves and the time from first adverse event (first month of negative cashflow, first month of falling reputation) to loss, which separates spirals from cliffs and tells the balance log which rule to dampen. Holmgård-style personas (a cash-hoarder, a growth-maximiser, a small-opening conservative) cost only a utility change and would show whether the Orchard punishes one opening or every opening ([arXiv 1802.06881](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.06881)). Supercell's "bots gravitated toward particular items" translates directly to income entropy: a bot whose income collapses onto classes and house calls has found the dominant strategy the audit describes ([PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/how-moco-used-ai-to-test-the-player-experience/)). Politowski's doubling-and-halving is the right first pass for each rule change, because small changes vanish into seed noise, and the Chimera practice of tuning to an intended rate rather than to "feels better" is what the audit already asks for when it says stars and profit should not come much sooner than before ([Google Research](https://www.research.google/blog/leveraging-machine-learning-for-game-development/)). Mega Crit's "super playtester" warning applies in reverse: a single scripted bot is one skill tier, and conclusions about "a reasonable opening" need at least a second one.

Two smaller findings close the loop with the audit's bug list. Two dialogs in one frame losing the first, a failed inspection's fine-or-bribe choice among them, is a channel-discipline failure of exactly the kind Factorio's team describes, where a must-know interruption is lost in noise ([Factorio FFF-342](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-342)); the save upgrade that marks every old month as "guests kept 0% of their patience" is Cook's "pretty but not functional" feedback, a readout that teaches a false cause ([Lost Garden](https://lostgarden.com/2012/07/01/building-tight-game-systems-of-cause-and-effect/)). And if a difficulty surface is ever wanted, the sourced pattern is one table of per-system knobs (payroll cushion, season strength, reputation dampening, demand coupling) with the shipped default as a row and a note, in the ONI manner, of which pressures the default will never relax ([ONI wiki](https://oxygennotincluded.wiki.gg/wiki/Game_Settings)); a commitment toggle such as "no reload after bankruptcy" belongs beside it, not inside it.

## Conclusion

The owner's constraint, communicate better rather than ease off, turns out to be the genre's settled position rather than a stricter one: the evidence says players like a game more when they have failed in it and can say why, and the sims that are praised as fair are the ones whose every loss was preceded by a graded, attributable, counterable signal. Two Point Hospital is the right benchmark for that grammar but the wrong one for its magnitudes; its own veterans show that +1/−2 reputation and a −$300,000 floor make the fail state decorative, and Pressure Point should keep its sharper teeth while adopting the two-stage warnings, the size-coupled demand, the tiered credit and the one-star gate that make Two Point feel fair. The novel point for this project is that the five headline problems are not five tuning jobs: the Orchard, the Boardwalk and the patience goals are all the reputation loop plus a line, seen through an interface that shows the symptom after the fact, and the demand coupling is what decides whether building is ever the right move.

What changes going forward is the order of work and the instrument. Prison Architect's sequence (make the economy flow, then enforce failure, with a two-condition trigger and a bounded grace) and the testing literature's two missing readings (a thoughtless baseline over the same seeds, and per-seed survival curves rather than means) give a way to make each change and prove it did not make the game easier. The gaps are worth naming: no management-sim-specific fairness study exists, no studio has published a seed-variance threshold, Frostpunk's ultimatum length and Two Point's arrival formula are player-reported, and the CorsixTH reputation constants are a clone's. None of those gaps touches the central claim, which rests on the shipped games as much as the theory: fair sims warn twice before they punish, and they tell the player what the second warning would have cost to heed.
