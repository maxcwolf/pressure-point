# Fair challenge in management sims: what design theory and practice say about challenge that feels fair, and about communicating system state

Scope note: primary designer writing was reachable for Soren Johnson (Designer Notes columns), Daniel Cook (Lost Garden), Keith Burgun (Game Developer), Jesper Juul (his own 2009 study), and Sid Meier (quoted keynote). Schell, Koster, Chen, Mark Brown, Extra Credits and the Frostpunk / Prison Architect developers were reachable only through secondary summaries, listings or wikis; each such item is flagged "(secondary)". Where a claim rests on playtest or study data rather than opinion it is flagged "(data)".

## Q1. What makes a difficulty spike feel unfair rather than hard, according to designers and research?

### Takeaway

Across designers and the one controlled study found, the dividing line is attribution: a loss feels fair when the player can trace it to a decision they made with information they had and could have countered; it feels unfair when the cause was hidden, arrived too late to react to, broke the link between decision and outcome, or looked like the game changing the rules. Players actively prefer feeling responsible for failure (Juul, data) and rate games lower when they blame the game.

### Cited Findings

- Juul's 2009 online study (85 players, Pac-Man/Snake prototype) asked "whether players prefer games where they do not feel responsible for failing"; players who blamed their own mistakes rated the game more positively than those who said the game was too hard (p<0.016). Juul concludes players "prefer feeling responsible for their own failure", and that not feeling responsible is tied to a negative perception of the game. (data) — [Juul, "Fear of Failing? The Many Meanings of Difficulty in Video Games"](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)
- Same study: players who finished without ever failing rated the game lower than players who lost lives and still won ("completing the game without failing was followed by a lower rating of the game", p=0.06, near-significant); players who called the game too easy described it as shallow because there was no need to rethink strategy. Juul also reports an "attribution asymmetry": players credit themselves for success more readily than they accept blame for failure. (data) — [Juul 2009](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)
- Juul's book The Art of Failure frames the "paradox of failure" (we avoid failure yet seek games that guarantee it) and uses Weiner's attribution theory: internal vs external (player's fault vs the game's), stable vs unstable (chance), global vs specific ("whether the player is deficient with no ability to improve, or just deficient in a particular skill"); mapping failure onto these axes indicates whether it feels fair and whether players keep trying or give up. (secondary, review by designer Liz England) — [Liz England review](https://lizengland.com/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/1947); publisher summary of the paradox — [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/neuropsych/play-video-games-to-fail/)
- Soren Johnson, "Our Cheatin' Hearts": "With cheating, perception becomes reality, so transparency is the antidote to suspicion and distrust." and "When the question is one of fairness, the player is always right." Hidden rule-breaking (AI units built under fog of war in early Civ) read as "how the computer was playing by different rules from the human"; explicit self-balancing rules (Mario Kart items, Catan's robber) "don't feel like cheating because the game is so explicit about how the system works." Small incremental AI bonuses in Civ drew little anger because "Their effect is too small to notice on a turn-by-turn basis", whereas granting a Wonder instantly "felt entirely different". (opinion grounded in shipped-game feedback) — [Designer Notes, GD Column 7](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=132)
- Johnson, "Challenging Design": "Players need to perceive that they are improving against a fixed measuring stick." Oblivion's level scaling "turned off many people" because it broke that stick; forced repetition of easy sections means "the game is punishing the player instead of challenging him"; and "if the player can see the invisible hand controlling the challenge, the spell is broken." He praises systems that put "the player in control, not the designer" (Thief's self-set objectives, Desktop Tower Defense's speed control, Civ4's One City Challenge / Always War options). (opinion) — [Designer Notes, GD Column 10](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=179)
- Johnson, "Playing the Odds": a hidden late "nasty surprise" means "the player will feel unprepared and cheated"; unlucky rolls "feel the fairest" when they come early enough that players have time to react; Civ4's help mode "showed the exact probability of success in combat" and this "drastically increased player satisfaction with the underlying mechanics". Also: players treat 1% as more common than it is and 99% as certainty; belief that odds "even out over time" (gambler's fallacy) is widespread. (opinion plus shipped-game observation) — [Designer Notes, GD Column 9](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=171)
- Sid Meier (GDC 2010 keynote, as quoted): "Players want to be in control. So, we learned that randomness has to be treated with a lot of care." "When random things happen, paranoia strikes the heart of the gamer." On combat odds: "Right around 3:1 to 4:1 area, the player felt like he or she should have the chance to win." On setbacks: "It's important to explain why these things happen, and how to avoid these things in the future." On expectations: "In games, you always win. I never get complaint letters from fans and gamers saying, 'I win too much.'" (opinion from Civ playtesting) — [VentureBeat quotes](https://venturebeat.com/business/quotes-from-sid-meiers-keynote-gdc-speech)
- Meier's GDC 2012 "Interesting Decisions" talk revisits "a game is a series of interesting decisions" and examines how to present decisions to players; it is free on GDC Vault but no transcript was reachable. — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-sid-meier-explores-interesting-decisions-in-gameplay); course reader pointing to the "Making Decisions More Interesting" segment (37:00-44:37) — [Chemeketa reader](https://computerscience.chemeketa.edu/cis125greader/Choice/BossBattle.html)
- Keith Burgun: "Output randomness is noise injected between the player's decision and the outcome." Input randomness "informs the player before he makes his decision." Output randomness severs the decision-outcome chain ("The tie has been severed, and we can no longer use my move as contextual nuance for our current game state") so a well-played game can still be lost; input randomness that lands too close to the decision is also bad: "It's up so close in your face that you don't have time to respond to it." Recommendation: "output randomness in all its forms is to be avoided" in strategy games; single-player games "require input randomness." (opinion) — [Burgun, "Randomness and Game Design"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/randomness-and-game-design); his "information horizon" refinement (limit information load, limit output randomness; fog of war gives "at least a few turns" to respond) — [keithburgun.net](https://keithburgun.net/james-lantz-challenge-to-the-information-horizon/)
- Johnson, "Water Finds a Crack": "given the opportunity, players will optimize the fun out of a game"; players pick "what gives the most reward for the least risk"; a dominant strategy removes choice because alternatives become "provably sub-optimal"; "The sweet spot for game design is when a specific decision is right in some circumstances but not in others." Civ4's per-city maintenance scaling with city count is the given fix for "infinite city sleaze". (opinion grounded in shipped Civ exploits) — [Designer Notes, GD Column 17](https://designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-17-water-finds-a-crack)
- Study of 3M+ online-game data points (ICIS): "self-failure increases, while team-failure decreases, player's likelihood to stay in the game", i.e. attribution, not difficulty alone, drives retention. (data; abstract only) — [University of Miami / ICIS](https://scholarship.miami.edu/esploro/outputs/conferenceProceeding/ICIS---The-Bright-Side-of/991032069202502976)
- Allart et al. (2017) tie attribution to design: failure is mainly attributable to player skill in Rayman Legends, while in The Division "the avatar's strength plays the key role". (academic) — [CNAM](https://cedric.cnam.fr/index2.php/publis/article/ALP17)
- Koster (secondary, user-compiled quotes): "Fun is just another word for learning"; "Noise is any pattern we don't understand"; "Boredom is the opposite of learning." — [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/author/quotes/10980.Raph_Koster); Koster credits Chris Crawford and originally phrased it as "fun is the emotional response to learning" — [Theory of Fun 10 Years Later PDF](https://www.raphkoster.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Theory-of-Fun-10-Years-Later.pdf)
- Extra Credits "Perfect Imbalance" (2012, 10 min) argues deliberate slight imbalance creates a shifting metagame so no single style is permanently "correct"; critics in the thread say strategy/counter-strategy cycles are hard to engineer because a counter must be nearly as widely adopted as the dominant strategy. (secondary; no transcript found) — [TheTVDB listing](https://thetvdb.com/series/extra-credits/episodes/4363017); [Riptide Lab discussion](https://riptidelab.com/forum/goto/post?id=46797)

### Inferences

- For a management sim, the practical test of a spike is: could an attentive player have (a) seen the cause in the UI before it hit, (b) understood which lever counters it, and (c) had enough turns/days for that lever to work? Fail any one and the loss is attributed to the game (Juul, Burgun, Johnson all converge here).
- Johnson's "fixed measuring stick" plus Burgun's "decision-outcome chain" implies that a sim whose rival/market strength silently scales with the player, or whose prices/odds are hidden, will feel unfair even when numerically easy.
- Meier's 3:1 observation and Johnson's 1%/99% point suggest showing exact odds or expected values rather than letting players infer them; a visible 75% success chance that fails is tolerated, an invisible one is not.

### Gaps

- No transcript of Meier's 2012 "Interesting Decisions" talk or of GMTK's difficulty episodes was reachable; GMTK material here is from episode listings and a third-party summary only.
- Raph Koster quotations are user-compiled (Goodreads) and should be checked against the book before being quoted in print.
- No dedicated academic study on perceived fairness of difficulty in management/simulation games specifically was found; Juul's data come from an arcade-style prototype.

## Q2. How much warning is enough before a loss, and what forms of warning work (icons, advisors, overlays, numbers, sound)?

### Takeaway

No source gives a universal number, but designers converge on a rule: the warning must arrive early enough that the player's countermeasure can take effect, and the cause must be shown, not just the symptom. In practice shipped sims use layered channels: an always-visible meter with rate-of-change and contributing factors (Prison Architect), twin meters with a sustained-threshold loss rather than an instant one (Frostpunk), and advisors/pop-ups that link to the problem's location (SimCity 4).

### Cited Findings

- Daniel Cook, "Building Tight Game Systems of Cause and Effect": players learn by forming and testing hypotheses; a system is "tight" when cause and effect are clearly linked. Factors that loosen it include long delays between cause and effect, indirection through secondary systems (SimEarth), hidden state (Mastermind), probabilistic outcomes, non-linearity and feedback that is "pretty but not functional". He names feedback problems the most common errors and prescribes: strengthen feedback, reduce noise, make "the most important information the center of attention", and let players set their own pace when learning complex systems. Also: the lowest-skill loops should be the tightest "since they serve as the gateway." (opinion from practice) — [Lost Garden](https://lostgarden.com/2012/07/01/building-tight-game-systems-of-cause-and-effect/)
- Cook on sensory channels: visual and tactile feedback is read more clearly than auditory; tight designs stack aligned channels (Peggle's particles, sound and time dilation) while loose ones rely on a single faint channel. — [Lost Garden](https://lostgarden.com/2012/07/01/building-tight-game-systems-of-cause-and-effect/)
- Burgun: input randomness must sit far enough ahead of the decision to be answerable; fog of war in roguelikes gives "at least a few turns to make inputs before they must react"; otherwise "Feedback is being artificially delayed." (opinion) — [keithburgun.net](https://keithburgun.net/james-lantz-challenge-to-the-information-horizon/); [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/randomness-and-game-design)
- Johnson: unlucky outcomes "feel the fairest" when players have time to react; Armageddon Empires put its dice on screen, which made chance "a tool for the player instead of a mystery"; design rule: "Avoid hidden penalties that players cannot learn to anticipate." (opinion) — [Designer Notes, GD Column 9](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=171)
- Prison Architect Alpha 10 (2013) replaced its anger system with a "Thermometer" showing the prison's danger level; a high temperature leads to fights; each prisoner has a "boiling point" (max-sec lower); the toolbar meter shows "the current temperature, rate of change and factors affecting the danger level". (secondary, fan wiki of the official alpha notes) — [Prison Architect Wiki, Alpha 10](https://prison-architect.fandom.com/wiki/Alpha_10)
- Frostpunk: Hope and Discontent sit at the bottom of the screen; if Discontent is too high people rebel, if Hope is too low people may leave; the game ends only if Hope is too low or Discontent too high "for an extended period of time", i.e. a sustained threshold rather than an instant trip. Banishment is the designed fail state, intended to make the player feel the consequences of harsh policies. (secondary: encyclopedia and reviews) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk); [Slant review](https://www.slantmagazine.com/games/review/frostpunk)
- Frostpunk designer Jakub Stokalski on the intended slow slide: "The basic concept that emerged from the production process was the analogy of the boiled frog: enough small, tenuous steps take us, as a society, to a very bad place." Decision mechanisms ask "what small concessions they're willing to make in order to ensure survival"; after a scenario the game summarizes choices and asks "Was it worth it?" (primary quote via interview) — [Gamepressure interview](https://www.gamepressure.com/editorials/fifth-dont-kill-on-moral-dilemmas-in-video-games/a-shard-of-ice-instead-of-a-heart/zf4b9); [TechRaptor summary](https://techraptor.net/content/how-11-bit-studios-created-society-survival-simulator-frostpunk)
- A Steam player complaint about Frostpunk describes a specific event as feeling unfair after a run of good management because it read as a sudden loss rather than something that built up with warning (single-user opinion, useful as a failure case). — [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1696045708646208587)
- SimCity advisor lineage: SimCity 2000 advisors "were poorly received by players as they only pop-up to give brief advice and complain"; SimCity 3000's were "much more developed"; in SimCity 4 "during an urgent crisis, the advisors will notify through a pop-up dialog box, which will provide a link to direct to the scene of emergency", on by default and switchable off; SimCity DS used a scrolling news ticker. (secondary, fan wiki) — [SimCity wiki](https://simcity.fandom.com/wiki/File:Ad.jpg); [SimCity DS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SimCity_DS)
- Civilization advisors: multiple advisors each focused on a sector; Civ II's High Council of five advisors in scripted video; Civ V's four-person team "provides in-game help and commentary on everything that's happening in your empire", e.g. the Military Advisor nags toward unit production. (secondary, fan wiki) — [Civilization Wiki: Advisor](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Advisor); [Advisers (Civ5)](<https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Advisers_(Civ5)>)
- Will Wright's MasterClass topics include "Build a Mental Model in Your Player's Head", "Human Behavior Is Derived From Mental Models" and "Allow Your Model to Diverge From Reality"; the course frames development as "as much about programming a player's brain as it is about programming the game itself." (secondary course listing) — [MasterClass](https://www.masterclass.com/classes/will-wright-teaches-game-design-and-theory/chapters/exploring-player-psychology)
- Schell's flow components for players (secondary summaries of The Art of Game Design): clear goals, limited distractions, hands and minds engaged, "direct and immediate feedback", continuous challenge between boredom and frustration. — [Notes by Lex](https://notesbylex.com/key-components-of-flow-for-players); [Baruch blog](https://blogs.baruch.cuny.edu/writersteachingwriters/writing-in-the-zone/)
- Schell, Lens of Challenge (#38): "What are the challenges in my game? Are they too easy, too hard, or just right? Can my challenges accommodate a wide variety of skill levels? How does the level of challenge increase as the player succeeds? What is the maximum level of challenge in my game?" Lens of Judgement asks "Do players feel the judgement is fair?" (secondary course page) — [CGDC course page](https://cgdc.org/?p=3900)

### Inferences

- "Enough warning" should be defined in the game's own time units as: warning lead time >= the time for the slowest reasonable counter to bite (hire, build, repay, research), plus one cycle for the player to notice. Frostpunk's sustained-threshold loss and Prison Architect's rate-of-change readout are two shipped encodings of this.
- The effective warning forms, in order of designer endorsement: (1) always-on meter with trend and causes; (2) exact numbers/odds on hover; (3) an advisor/notification that names the cause and links to it; (4) sound as a secondary cue only (Cook ranks audio below visual/tactile). SimCity 2000's reception warns against advisors that only complain without a lever.
- Cook's "pacing" factor suggests a slow decline needs a trend indicator precisely because the delay between cause and effect is long; the symptom alone (a lower number) will not be attributed correctly.

### Gaps

- No primary developer statement from 11 bit studios on Frostpunk's warning thresholds was reachable (PC Gamer and TechRaptor interviews returned 403/truncated); the sustained-threshold claim rests on Wikipedia and reviews.
- No Introversion dev-diary text for Prison Architect's thermometer was reachable (wiki page returned 402); the factor list is incomplete.
- No quantitative research on optimal warning lead time in strategy/management games was found.

## Q3. What feedback should accompany a slow decline (reputation, finances, morale) so the player can act in time?

### Takeaway

Designers agree that a slow decline must be made legible as a trend with attributable causes and a visible counter: show the rate of change, the contributing factors, and the lever, and surface the first warning while the player still has slack. Advisors that "complain" without pointing at a lever were disliked (SimCity 2000); meters that show direction and drivers (Prison Architect) and odds/costs shown explicitly (Civ4) are the praised pattern.

### Cited Findings

- Cook: indirection and long delays are what make a loop illegible; "Discrete or low-value states" are tighter than analog high-range values; "Make the most important information the center of attention, and remove distracting elements." Tune using observed play from prototypes. (opinion) — [Lost Garden](https://lostgarden.com/2012/07/01/building-tight-game-systems-of-cause-and-effect/)
- Johnson, "Taking Feedback" column exists but concerns player feedback to designers, not in-game feedback; it is listed for completeness. — [Designer Notes writings index](https://www.designer-notes.com/writings/)
- Johnson's Civ3 note: random resource clustering left players "unable to field armies" and complaining; Civ4 constrained placement of critical resources so the shortage was not perceived as arbitrary. (shipped-game observation) — [Designer Notes, GD Column 9](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=171)
- Johnson on overflow micromanagement: when Civ wasted surplus beakers/hammers, players re-tuned tax rates and citizens every turn; Civ4 applied overflow to the next item, removing the tedious loop. Lesson: watch for rules that "inadvertently encourage" time-for-safety grinding. — [Designer Notes, GD Column 17](https://designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-17-water-finds-a-crack)
- Prison Architect's meter exposes "rate of change and factors affecting the danger level", not only the level. (secondary) — [Prison Architect Wiki, Alpha 10](https://prison-architect.fandom.com/wiki/Alpha_10)
- Frostpunk pairs two opposing meters so the player reads a tension rather than a single health bar; reviews describe the HUD summarising "population's discontent, hope, labour utilisation, health and pressing concerns" and credit "small audio and visual cues" for events such as laws passing or blizzards. (secondary) — [Checkpoint Gaming review](https://checkpointgaming.net/reviews/2018/04/frost-punk-review-not-winter-wonderland/); Frostpunk 2 was criticised when laws became "tiny buttons relegated to the corner" and the studio promised a HUD that is "clearer and more intuitive" — [PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/frostpunk-2/ui-improvements)
- Meier: "It's important to explain why these things happen, and how to avoid these things in the future." — [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/quotes-from-sid-meiers-keynote-gdc-speech)
- Loss aversion evidence: a Zelda-style experiment found that "despite the temporary and digital nature of the game world, and the virtual nature of the gold, players still exhibited a strong bias towards avoiding losses." (data) — [University of Saskatchewan thesis](https://harvest.usask.ca/handle/10388/13478); Engelstein's Achievement Relocked treats loss aversion as the different reactions to gaining a reward vs losing something as punishment, and notes designers usually want to avoid triggering it "but not always". (secondary review) — [Museum of Play review](https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/05/14-1-Book-Review-5.pdf)
- Learned helplessness: Juul's framework (via England) defines it as "the lack of belief in your own competence when faced with the possibility of failure"; a 2018 paper argues that misinforming players about their influence on outcomes produces learned helplessness. (secondary/academic) — [Liz England](https://lizengland.com/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/1947); [Failed Failures, UMCS](https://journals.umcs.pl/nh/article/view/6330)

### Inferences

- For a declining stat the minimum legible package is: current value, direction/rate, top contributing causes (positive and negative), the threshold that matters, and a link to the screen where the counter lives. This is a synthesis of Cook's factors, Prison Architect's meter and SimCity 4's linked pop-ups.
- Because of loss aversion, framing the same number as "you are losing X per week" will land harder than "you earn Y less"; use the loss framing for warnings you want acted on, and avoid it for noise.
- A decline that cannot be traced to a specific cause invites learned helplessness and external attribution; "balanced" numbers do not rescue an illegible cause.

### Gaps

- No source quantified how many contributing factors a meter should display before it becomes noise; Cook's advice ("3 upgrades instead of 60") is about options, not readouts.
- GMTK's episode S04E01 "How Games Use Feedback" (9 Jan 2018) is confirmed to exist but no transcript was reachable; only the Mario Kart item-distribution example is confirmed via a third-party summary. — [TheTVDB](https://thetvdb.com/series/game-makers-toolkit/episodes/7730772); [Digikala summary (Persian)](https://www.digikala.com/mag/gmtk-feedback-loop/)

## Q4. How should failure states be designed so they teach rather than end the game, and when is a hard game-over right?

### Takeaway

The consensus is that failure should carry information and a path back: Juul's data show failure "adds content" and that players who never failed liked the game less, while Johnson warns against setback punishment that forces replaying what was already mastered. A hard game-over is defended when the game's theme is the consequence itself (Frostpunk's banishment, Blow's call to embrace failure and tragedy) and when the player has been shown the cause and had the chance to act.

### Cited Findings

- Juul 2009: failure makes players reconsider strategy and so "adds content"; the energy-punishment variant did not make players like the game more, and Juul suggests casual games' appeal comes from sparing "setback punishment", such as forced replays; "single-player players rarely seek extra challenge on their own, so designers must build challenge in." (data) — [Juul, Fear of Failing](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)
- Juul's goal taxonomy for designing failure: "completable goals that can be reached only once", transient goals that can be repeated, and improvement goals measured against one's last performance. (secondary) — [Liz England](https://lizengland.com/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/1947)
- Johnson: repeating easy sections means "the game is punishing the player instead of challenging him"; Ninja Gaiden Black offered an easier mode after repeated deaths and God of War later adopted it; Call of Duty 4 used its training level to suggest a difficulty; Left 4 Dead adjusts spawns and drops dynamically. (opinion) — [Designer Notes, GD Column 10](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=179)
- Schell, Lens of Punishment (#47): "What are the punishments in my game? Why am I punishing the players? What do I hope to achieve by it? Do my punishments seem fair to the players? Why or why not? Is there a way to turn these punishments into rewards and get the same or a better effect? Are my strong punishments balanced against commensurately strong rewards?" (primary question set via Schell Games blog / course page) — [Schell Games blog](https://schellgames.com/blog/the-lens-of-punishment); [CGDC](https://cgdc.org/?p=3900)
- Jonathan Blow, "Design Reboot" (MIGS 2007): games "will need to embrace the dynamics of failure, tragedy, comedy and romance. They will need to stop pandering to the player's desire for mastery"; he called scheduled-reward design unethical and argued for rewards that are "intellectually nutritious and not simply addictive". (primary talk, via Koster's and Tale of Tales' write-ups) — [Raph Koster on Design Reboot](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=1529); [Tale of Tales](https://tale-of-tales.com/blog/2007/12/09/jonathan-blows-design-reboot/)
- Blow's Braid: he asked what would change if players had unlimited rewind and did not have to fear dying; all puzzles were tied to rewind, so failure is a mechanic rather than a fail state. (secondary retrospective) — [Game Developer, "Braid Behind the Scenes"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/braid-behind-the-scenes); essay noting Braid still frustrates because puzzles can stump players despite no death penalty — [Critical Video Game Studies](https://criticalvideogamestudies.com/challenging-play-and-why-braid-frustrates-so-many/)
- Frostpunk's banishment ending is read by reviewers as deliberate: the workers banish you because "they've had enough of your management", and the game is meant to make the player feel the consequences of harsh policies; the post-scenario "Was it worth it?" summary turns the ending into reflection. (secondary; designer quote on intent above) — [Slant](https://www.slantmagazine.com/games/review/frostpunk); [Gamepressure](https://www.gamepressure.com/editorials/fifth-dont-kill-on-moral-dilemmas-in-video-games/a-shard-of-ice-instead-of-a-heart/zf4b9)
- Stokalski on irreversibility: certain government changes are "irreversible decisions, a point of no return" (Path of Order and Discipline), i.e. the game signals irreversibility explicitly rather than letting the player discover it. (primary quote via dev video summary) — [TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/how-11-bit-studios-created-society-survival-simulator-frostpunk)
- Meier: "In games, you always win"; his stated policy became letting the player win with the threat of punishment keeping things interesting; "the player is looking for a satisfactory conclusion." (opinion) — [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/quotes-from-sid-meiers-keynote-gdc-speech); [Engadget summary](https://www.engadget.com/2010-03-14-sid-meier-talks-player-psychology-and-the-year-of-civilization.html)
- Jenova Chen's thesis "Flow in Games" (USC) argued for dynamic difficulty adjustment where the player chooses: "allowing players to make their own choice within the game to alter the difficulty can improve the enjoyment they experience"; published as "Flow in games (and everything else)", CACM 50(4), 2007. (secondary; PDF not text-extractable here) — [Wikipedia, Flow (video game)](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(video_game)>); [jenovachen.com](https://www.jenovachen.com/flowingames/)
- Hunicke and Chapman's "Hamlet" DDA system for Half-Life adjusted parameters reactively and proactively, with reportedly unclear results; Ang and Mitchell (CHI PLAY 2017) compared DDA systems' effects on experience. (academic, abstracts only) — [arXiv survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.04471)

### Inferences

- A management-sim failure state teaches when it (a) names the cause in the game's own terms, (b) shows what would have countered it, and (c) returns the player to a point where that counter is still available (autosave before the slide, a grace period, or a "demoted but playing" state). Frostpunk's sustained threshold and Johnson's "invisible hand" warning both argue for the grace period being visible, not silent rubber-banding.
- Hard game-over is defensible when (1) the theme is consequence, (2) the run is short enough that restart is cheap, or (3) the player opted into it (ironman); otherwise Juul's and Johnson's evidence favours setbacks that preserve mastered progress.
- Blow's position and Juul's "paradox" together imply that removing all failure (an unlosable sim) also harms engagement; the target is failure that is attributable and recoverable, not absent.

### Gaps

- No primary source from Blow on punishment/permadeath specifically; "Design Reboot" is about failure as theme, not fail-state mechanics.
- No reachable study comparing game-over vs setback in management/sim genres; Juul's punishment comparison was in an arcade prototype.
- Chen's thesis text could not be extracted (PDF binary); the eight flow components and passive/active DDA distinction are not quoted here.

## Q5. What do designers say about asymmetric penalties (a failure costing more than a success earns) and about negative feedback loops that stabilise a game vs positive ones that spiral?

### Takeaway

Designers treat positive loops as the engine of runaway wins and death spirals, and negative loops as the stabiliser, but warn that hidden stabilisers read as cheating; the fix is to make the loop explicit (Mario Kart items, Catan's robber, Civ4's city maintenance). Asymmetric penalties are tolerated when visible and small per tick (Johnson's Civ discounts), and resented when they arrive as a lump the player could not foresee; loss aversion means an equal-sized loss already feels larger than the matching gain.

### Cited Findings

- Positive feedback: "the player who's winning gets resources that help them win more, so a lead compounds"; Catan settlements producing resources that fund more settlements is the standard example. Negative loops "make it harder for a player who is doing well to continue pulling away from the field and easier for a player doing poorly to get back in the game"; rubber-banding in racing games; rising RPG level-up costs that stop grinding "to level 99 in 20 minutes". Options: dampen a strong loop (Call of Duty killstreaks were tuned down) or decouple score from future scoring, which "requires careful tuning of positive feedback and negative feedback". Well-balanced games "blend the two". (teaching text / design blog) — [Chemeketa reader, Feedback Loops](https://computerscience.chemeketa.edu/cis125greader/MechanicsDynamics/FeedbackLoops.html); [Games Precipice, Positional Balance](https://www.gamesprecipice.com/positional-balance); [Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/positive-and-negative-feedback-loops-in-game-design)
- Johnson: self-balancing rules "don't feel like cheating because the game is so explicit about how the system works"; hidden rubber-banding "is often obvious to the players" and provokes distrust; in asymmetrical games cheating is "simply bad game design"; proposed Puzzle Quest fix: cheat "only in favor of the player". (opinion from practice) — [Designer Notes, GD Column 7](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=132)
- Johnson's example of a negative loop installed to kill a positive one: Civ4's "per-city maintenance cost that scaled with the total number of cities" to stop infinite city sprawl. — [Designer Notes, GD Column 17](https://designer-notes.com/game-developer-column-17-water-finds-a-crack)
- Johnson, "Less Than Zero (on zero-sum mechanics)" is listed in his column index as the piece on zero-sum design; not fetched. — [Designer Notes writings index](https://www.designer-notes.com/writings/)
- Johnson on hidden vs visible asymmetry: Civ's AI discounts were tolerated because their effect was "too small to notice on a turn-by-turn basis", while an instant Wonder grant "felt entirely different"; Puzzle Quest drew suspicion over AI drop rates even at low odds because "the human mind is notoriously bad at grasping probability." — [Designer Notes, GD Column 7](http://www.designer-notes.com/?p=132)
- Juul's "attribution asymmetry": players credit themselves for success more than they accept blame for failure, so a symmetric reward/penalty is experienced asymmetrically. (data) — [Juul 2009](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)
- Loss aversion in games: players showed "a strong bias towards avoiding losses" even for virtual gold; designers should account for it in risk/reward structures. (data) — [University of Saskatchewan](https://harvest.usask.ca/handle/10388/13478); Engelstein frames rewards vs punishments through loss aversion and notes designers "most of the time" want to avoid triggering it. — [Museum of Play review](https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/05/14-1-Book-Review-5.pdf)
- Schell's Lens of Punishment asks directly: "Are my strong punishments balanced against commensurately strong rewards?" and "Is there a way to turn these punishments into rewards and get the same or a better effect?" — [Schell Games blog](https://schellgames.com/blog/the-lens-of-punishment)
- Meier on randomness that reads as punishment: players "felt that the AI was ganging up on the human" in early Civ when the AI declared war on a leader by 1900 AD, even though humans would do the same in multiplayer; Civ4 "intentionally crippled the AI's ability to trade with one another" to avoid a cartel perception. ### Inferences

- For a single-player management sim the relevant spiral is the death spiral (low cash -> no hires -> lower income -> lower cash). The designer literature implies installing an explicit negative loop (cheaper recovery when small, bank grace period, rising costs with scale) and surfacing it in the UI, rather than a hidden rubber-band that players will detect and resent.
- An asymmetric penalty is acceptable when it is (1) announced in advance as a rule, (2) small per tick so it is not read as a lump, and (3) attributable to a player decision; a penalty that is numerically fair but lands as one unforeseen lump will be judged unfair under loss aversion and attribution asymmetry.
- Johnson's "cheat only in favor of the player" and Meier's "players want to be in control" point to making stabilisers player-visible choices (loans, bailouts, difficulty toggles) rather than silent adjustments.
